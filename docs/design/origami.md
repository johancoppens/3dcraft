# Origami: crease patterns, rigid folding, Kawasaki and Maekawa

A crease pattern on a flat sheet, folded rigidly by its fold angles: `feature.fold` (the document's `Fold`), `fold.edit`, `fold.inspect`, `fold.check`, and the crease pattern through `file.export` with `fold`. The kernel's part is `craft3d_kernel::origami` (`crates/kernel/src/origami/`), regeneration's `crates/regen/src/fold.rs`, the commands `crates/engine/src/fold_cmds.rs`. Built 2026-10-26 (R1 block *sheets*, item 4: `plan/r1-sheets.md`).

## Method

**The sheet and the creases.** A fold reads one sketch. The crease lines are lines of it (drawn or construction), each a *mountain*, a *valley* or a *cut*; the sheet is bounded by the closed profiles of the sketch's other curves (`detect_profiles`, so holes and several pieces come for free), which must be straight lines. Seen from the side the sketch's normal points to, a valley folds the paper towards the viewer; its fold angle counts positive, a mountain's negative (Tachi's convention). A given angle is the magnitude, 0 (flat) to 180 degrees (folded flat); the kind says which way.

**Facets: the planar arrangement** (`arrange.rs`). The boundary pieces and the creases are one set of segments; every segment is split where another crosses it (a proper crossing, both parameters further than the tolerance from the ends) or ends on it (an end within the tolerance of the other segment). Points within the tolerance of each other are one vertex (chained, sorted by x; a cluster wider than four tolerances is refused as finer than the tolerance). Two segments that touch the same stretch (two of the points where one touches the other more than a tolerance apart) lie along each other and are refused. The faces are traced on the half-edges: round each vertex the half-edges that leave it are sorted counter-clockwise, and after a half-edge comes the one before its twin, so every face is walked with its inside on the left. A counter-clockwise cycle bounds a face; each connected piece of the graph has one clockwise cycle, its outside, which is a hole of the smallest counter-clockwise cycle *of another piece* around it (asked of other pieces only: a cycle of the same piece shares its vertices), or the outside of everything. A face lies in the sheet when its boundary pieces run the way the sheet's loops run (the sheet on their left) and is dropped when they run against them; a face bounded by creases alone lies in the sheet, because every piece of a crease is checked first to lie inside the sheet (its middle, by even-odd). The checks a crease pattern must pass, each by name: inside the sheet; dividing what it crosses (a crease with the same facet on both sides ends inside a facet, *dangling*; a cut doing so is a *slit*); no cycle of zero area (a crease floating free); no facet thinner than the tolerance (area at most the tolerance times its perimeter). Each fold crease piece between two facets is a *hinge*.

**Rigid folding** (`solve.rs`). Each facet moves as a rigid body. A breadth-first forest from the fixed facet (the largest, or the one holding `fixed`), then from the first facet of each other piece of a cut sheet, places every facet by the product of the rotations along its path, `T_child = T_parent ∘ R(crease, ±ρ)`, each rotation about the crease as it lies in the flat sheet (−ρ when the parent is on the crease's left). Every hinge outside the forest closes a loop and holds only when its two facets agree: `T_right = T_left ∘ R(crease, −ρ)`. This is the loop-closure condition of rigid origami (the product of the fold rotations round a loop is the identity: Belcastro and Hull's condition, Demaine and O'Rourke 2007, §13.1), here for every fundamental cycle of the facet graph, so it holds for any sheet: one vertex or many, with holes, cuts and several pieces, without asking which loops go round a vertex. Each loop is written as three points of the right facet (the crease's ends and a point beside it) that must land in the same place both ways round: nine numbers per loop, lengths, all zero when the loop closes.

Angles that are given are kept; the others are solved by Levenberg–Marquardt on those numbers, with the exact derivative: turning crease `k` by `dρ` moves a point `X` beyond it by `s D × (X − P) dρ`, `P` and `D` the crease's place and direction as its parent facet has it, `s` its sign in the forest. The normal equations are gathered row by row from the rows' few non-zero entries (a row depends only on the creases on the paths of its two facets), and solved dense with partial pivoting up to 400 unknowns (`MAX_DENSE`), from 32 unknowns only where the sparse solve below would not be under a quarter of its cost. The solve works about the middle of the sheet's box and its stopping rule and penalty are scaled by the box's diagonal, so a sheet far from the sketch's origin folds as one at the origin. **The fold is the continuation from the flat sheet**, and that is part of what is accepted. The given angles are approached in steps, the first of 1 degree of the largest, then doubling up to 10 degrees. The first step starts at the flat sheet, where the branches of a vertex meet (a flat-foldable degree-4 vertex has two, the modes below) and there is no tangent: every unknown crease is turned its kind's way by 1, 3, 10, 30 and 100 times the step (at most 170 degrees) as guesses, and a solution is taken when it closes, keeps every kind and lies on a branch through the flat sheet: solved again at half the step it is half as far, within a quarter (a smooth branch through the flat sheet is straight near it, whatever its slope). Every guess is tried; when two different solutions both pass, the kinds do not tell the branches apart and the fold is refused as *ambiguous*, naming the creases that differ (give one of them an angle). A joint without a kind (sign 0, which the linkage allows) is guessed both ways, every pattern up to six such joints. No crease pattern with mountains and valleys assigned was found where two branches through the flat sheet keep every kind (a search of random degree-6 vertices with three angles given found none), so the test is a degree-4 vertex whose solved creases have no kind. Every later step is predicted along the tangent of the branch, `J_free dx = −J_given dg` at the last solution (least squares, the normal equations' free block), and corrected by Levenberg–Marquardt from the prediction; it is taken when the corrector closes, keeps every kind and lands within a quarter of the predicted motion (plus the angular tolerance). A step not taken is halved, at most 24 times in a row (4 for the first step: near the flat sheet the branches are straight, so a smaller first step finds no other), and then the fold fails as *could not be followed from the flat sheet* (`NotFollowed`). Where `J_free` is singular partway (a bifurcation, or a branch that stops) there is no tangent, and the fold fails as *undetermined*, naming the creases (more freedom than the given angles fix, or a bifurcation). Nothing compares how fast the solved creases move with the given ones: a vertex whose solved creases fold fifty times as fast near the flat sheet (mode B at sectors of 61 and 59 degrees) and a Miura sheet of 88-degree parallelograms (1 / cos γ ≈ 29) fold by their closed forms. A solved crease turned against its kind also costs the sheet's size per radian in the least squares (zero at every solution that keeps the kinds). The whole solve has a budget of work (`MAX_WORK`, 2·10¹⁰ multiplications of the factorisations and residuals, some 20 seconds at the worst): past it the fold fails (`TooMuchWork`), so regeneration is bounded in time; 60 iterations per step. At the end the solution must close within the tolerance (else *not rigidly foldable at these angles*, naming the worst loop, or *did not converge*); the derivative's columns, scaled to unit length, must have full rank (a symmetric pivoted elimination; a pivot below 1e-10 leaves its crease *undetermined*, named), **also at the flat sheet**: with no angle given, or every given angle 0, solved creases are undetermined (at the flat sheet the branches meet and nothing says which way the others go), and the fold fails rather than lie flat; every solved crease must turn its kind's way within the angular tolerance, its angle wrapped into (−180, 180] degrees first. A solved crease on no loop at all (a bridge of the facet graph) is refused before solving. A crease line with an angle folds every piece of it by that angle; without one, each piece is its own unknown (a line through several vertices need not fold alike along its length).

**One fold mechanism** (`solve.rs`: `Linkage`, `Joint`, `fold_linkage`). The solve knows bodies and joints, not crease patterns: each joint has its own axis in space (a point, a unit direction, a side to sample the loop at, a reach), and the motion of its right body relative to its left one at angle ρ, `Joint::motion` (a turn about the axis by −ρ, after the shift along the neutral fibre for a joint with a radius), with its velocity `Joint::velocity`; `origami::fold` builds the linkage of an arrangement (axes in the sheet's plane, about the box's middle) and maps the motions back. A bend of sheet metal is a joint with a radius `r = R + K T` and a direction `û` across its strip (built 2026-10-27, [`sheetmetal.md`](sheetmetal.md) › *Folding*): its motion is `Rot(C(ρ), −ρ) ∘ Shift(−|ρ| r û)` with the axis `C` `r` from the strip's start on the side the body turns to, its velocity the turn's plus the turned shift's; a crease is the joint with `r = 0`; the forest, the loop closure, the continuation, the rank and the kinds' rule stay as they are, so folding a crease pattern and bending a part are one mechanism. Owed to `r1-plates` beside it: (b) the input is a sketch whose sheet is bounded by straight lines (`regen/src/fold.rs`, the boundary and the creases read from one sketch); a plate's outline and bend lines from faces or from an unfolded developable need their own reader; (c) `folded_sheet` builds a zero-thickness sheet whose names are keyed to sketch curve ids; a plate needs the solid between two offset sheets, with the bend zones as cylindrical faces.

**A sparse solve** (`sparse.rs`, built 2026-10-28). Past 400 unknowns (`MAX_DENSE`), and from 32 unknowns (`SPARSE_FROM`) wherever its factor costs less than a quarter of the dense one (`n³/3`), the loops are not the forest's fundamental cycles written in the fixed body's frame (a loop's rows then depend on every joint on the paths of its two bodies from the root, and the normal equations of a large sheet are dense) but a short cycle basis, each loop a chain of bodies from the left body of its closing joint (at the identity) to the right one, its residual the closing joint's three sample points carried both ways round: the same lengths (a rigid motion of a whole loop does not change them), now depending only on the loop's own joints. The basis is the shortest cycle through each joint (breadth first, without the joint), shortest first, kept when independent over GF(2) of those kept, completed by the fundamental cycles, so it spans every loop of any graph (holes, cuts, pieces); in a crease pattern the shortest cycle through a crease is the fan round one of its vertices. The normal equations are ordered by reverse Cuthill–McKee (Cuthill and McKee 1969; George and Liu 1981, §4.3) and factored as a skyline `L D Lᵀ`, all fill lying within each row's envelope (George and Liu §4.2); at most 2·10⁷ entries (`MAX_PROFILE`, 160 MB a copy; with the copies a step holds, the normal equations, the factor and the scaled ones of the rank test, about 0.5 GB at the cap), counted in the work budget. The rank test is the same factorisation of the unit-scaled equations: for a positive semi-definite matrix a zero pivot in any symmetric order is a column that depends on the earlier ones (a Schur complement of a semi-definite matrix is semi-definite, so a zero diagonal has a zero row), and a pivot below 1e-10 leaves its crease undetermined, as the dense elimination does (which creases are named can differ from the dense elimination's, which pivots on the largest). Up to 400 unknowns the dense solve is kept as it was. `MAX_FREE` is 16384. Measured on the Strix in release (`a_large_miura_sheet_folds_by_the_sparse_solve`): a Miura sheet of 16 × 16 cells (479 angles solved) 1.6–2.0 s dense, 0.03–0.04 s sparse; 24 × 24 (1103) 0.15 s; 40 × 40 (3119) 0.91 s, every angle to its closed form within 1e-8.

**Slits and slots** (2026-10-28). A cut with one facet on both sides is built: a *slit* (from the boundary, a crease or another cut, ending inside the facet) is part of the facet's outer loop, which runs along both its sides; a *slot* (a cut or a tree of cuts touching nothing) is a cycle of no area made of cuts alone, made a hole of the facet that holds it (the smallest counter-clockwise cycle of another piece round its first point, as any hole). On the sheet each side is an edge (pieces `2i`, `2i + 1`, as a cut's two sides), the slit's mouth two vertices and its tip one (fans, as before). A slit changes no motion (the facet is rigid); the contact test and the layers' overlay take its edges as they come (even-odd tests count a doubled edge twice; the overlay's folded edges of a slit or a slot are cuts there, so one may end inside a cell; every other folded edge must still divide). A fold crease ending inside a facet is still *dangling*.

**Thick panels** (`thick.rs`, 2026-10-28). `panels` on a fold builds the *membrane* model of thick origami (Zirbel et al. 2013 compare it with others): one prism per facet, `thickness` thick on the sketch's back or front, its outline set back by `clearance` from every crease and cut it lies on (not the boundary), the hinges on the sheet. The set-back is the mitred offset of the facet's loops, each edge by its own distance, refused (naming the facet) when an edge would turn round or vanish, the loops cross, a hole would fall outside, or two collinear edges have different set-backs or turn back on each other (a crease running straight into the boundary, a slit). The kinematics are the sheet's exactly; what thickness adds is room. Beside a crease folded towards the panels, the corner of one panel at the clearance and the far face reaches the other when `c (1 + cos ρ) = T sin ρ`, at `ρ = 2 atan(c / T)`; folded away they meet only at 180 degrees. Every pair of panels whose boxes meet is checked as solids: every face of one against every face of the other with the zero-thickness facet test (a face of a prism shares nothing with any other), and one corner of each inside the other (the one way two solids meet with no faces meeting); any meeting is refused naming the facets. A clearance of no more than the tolerance is refused (the panels would touch along every crease). The membrane facets are still checked as before. Names are an extrusion's per panel, each outline segment keyed by its curve and `2 piece + side` of its half-edge, the caps numbered when two panels have the same curves. Not built: the offset-panel technique (Edmondson et al. 2014) and tapered panels (Tachi 2011), which move the hinges off the sheet to fold thick panels flat and change the kinematics.

**A stack folded on** (`stack.rs`, 2026-10-28). When some given angles are 180 degrees and another given one is neither 0 nor 180, the 180-degree creases join their facets rigidly face over, each such group a *stack*, placed as the pattern of those creases alone (with the cuts) folds flat: that pattern is arranged and folded flat by reflections and its layers ordered (`fold_flat`, `layer_order`), and each facet of the full pattern takes the place and the layer of the facet it lies in. The other creases are the joints of a linkage of the stacks: a piece between facets `a` and `b` (placed alike by the flat state, `R_a = R_b`, since that crease is not folded there) turns the stack of `b` against the stack of `a` by its own angle about the crease where the flat state put it: from `T_f = B_stack(f) ∘ R_f` and `T_b = T_a ∘ M(ρ)`, `B_b = B_a ∘ R_a M(ρ) R_a⁻¹`, the turn by the same angle about `R_a(axis)`. The linkage is folded as any (given and solved angles, the continuation from the stacks lying flat, the rank and the kinds), and every facet is placed by `T_f = B_stack(f) ∘ R_f`. The 180-degree creases must divide the sheet and fold flat on their own: one that ends on a crease folded by less, or a vertex where they do not meet again without the others, is refused naming the 180-degree crease and why (`StackNotFlat`). A solved crease that would have to turn against its kind (it lies face over in its stack) is named with its kind (`StackKind`: found by solving the stacks again with the solved creases free to turn either way). A crease on some layers of a stack only is a joint with the same stack on both sides, which closes only at 0: *not rigidly foldable*, naming it, as paper that would have to bend without a crease. Contact: facets of one stack keep the flat state's layers and are not compared; facets of different stacks may not meet, except along a stretch where both have an edge of a crease that folds the stacks (the layers of a stack bend round one line; in the zero-thickness model they touch there). The layers reported (`fold.inspect`: `stacked`, the facets' layers and `above` the pairs of one stack that overlap, by an interior point of one in the other or edges that cross) are the stacks'; the picture lifts each stack's layers along its own normal. The body is the folded sheet, its stacked faces coincident by design, treated as a flat fold's (nothing built from it, no mesh-level self-intersection search). A given 180 beside given 0s only is still `MixedFlat`.

**Closed forms** (the oracles). A flat-foldable degree-4 vertex with sector angles α, β, π − α, π − β (counter-clockwise from crease 1) has two modes (Huffman 1976; Tachi 2009): mode A, ρ₃ = ρ₁, ρ₄ = −ρ₂, tan(ρ₂/2) = −(cos((α+β)/2) / cos((α−β)/2)) tan(ρ₁/2); mode B, ρ₃ = −ρ₁, ρ₄ = ρ₂, tan(ρ₂/2) = (sin((α+β)/2) / sin((α−β)/2)) tan(ρ₁/2). The Miura-ori vertex (sectors γ, π − γ, π − γ, γ: α + β = π) folds in mode B: the straight crease pieces by ±ρ, the zigzag ones by 2 atan(tan(ρ/2) / cos γ), γ the parallelograms' acute angle. Both were checked numerically before use (a closure product of rotation matrices solved by `scipy`, 2026-10-26, scratch).

**Contact** (`collide.rs`). No feature hands on a body that passes through itself or touches itself ([`regeneration.md`](regeneration.md) › *Never through itself*), so a fold whose facets meet other than where the pattern joins them fails, naming the two facets, and the existing `overlap` measure (a volume in common) has nothing to say about a sheet. Two facets in planes that cross meet only on the planes' common line `L`: each facet's part of `L` is a set of intervals (where its boundary crosses `L`, and between those points where the middle lies in the facet, closed), and the facets meet where their intervals overlap after what the pattern shares is taken out (the stretch of a shared edge, a tolerance round each shared vertex; the two sides of a cut share its edge and vertices). What is left is *through* where its middle lies inside both facets (further than the tolerance from their edges) and longer than the tolerance, *touch* otherwise. Two facets in one plane (normals within the angular tolerance, every corner within the tolerance of the other's plane) meet when a corner of one that is not shared lies in the other (inside or within the tolerance of its edges), or when their edges cross properly away from the shared vertices: below 180 degrees a sheet of no thickness may not lie on itself, so this is a touch. At 180 degrees the sheet is folded flat and the check is the order of the layers instead (*Folded flat*, below). The pairs looked at are those whose boxes (grown by the tolerance) meet, found by the kernel's box tree (`boxtree.rs`), in index order; the first meeting is the error. A facet that cannot be placed is an error too, never "no meeting" (fail closed). Parallel means the sine of the angle between the normals is at most the angular tolerance.

**The body** (`sheet.rs`). One planar face per facet (its plane the sketch's plane carried by the facet's motion, facing the way the sketch's normal is carried), one straight edge per hinge shared by its two facets, one per piece of the boundary and one per side of a cut piece. Round a vertex the facets meet in *fans*, runs joined by fold creases and ended by the boundary or a cut; each fan is one vertex of the body, so the boundary passes each vertex once and the pieces of a cut sheet share nothing. One shell per piece. The body passes `topo::check` as a sheet. Names are a fill's (`extrude::fill`, [`names.md`](names.md)): a facet `F7:startCap(e1,e5,e8)` after the sorted curves of its outer boundary (a piece number when two facets have the same), an edge `F7:startEdge(e5)` with its piece counted along the curve's own direction (a cut's two sides are pieces 2i and 2i + 1), a vertex `F7:startVertex(e1,e5)` after the curves meeting there and its fan.

**Folded flat** (`flat.rs`, `layers.rs`; built 2026-10-27). When at least one angle is given and every given angle is within the angular tolerance of 180 degrees (`folds_flat`), the fold is the *flat-folded state*: every fold crease, given or solved, folds by 180 degrees its kind's way (the limit of the rigid folds of a flat-foldable pattern, where every crease reaches 180 at once: tan(ρ/2) → ∞ in every closed form above). A given 180 beside a given angle that is not is refused (`MixedFlat`, naming the crease): facets folded onto each other are layered only in the flat state; a stack folded on by other angles is owed. *Placement.* A fold of 180 degrees about a line in the plane is the half-turn about it: within the plane the reflection across the line, the facet face over. Each facet's place is the product of the reflections along its path in the breadth-first forest from the fixed facet (`T_child = R(crease) then T_parent`), each half-turn built as the product of two mirrors (across the vertical plane through the crease, across the sheet's plane), so the plane's z is kept exactly (0, not 1e-16) and nothing is solved; mountain and valley land in the same place. A crease off the forest closes a loop and must bring its two facets to the same place within the tolerance at its ends and at a point its length beside it; round a vertex that is Kawasaki's condition (the product of the reflections round a vertex is the identity exactly when the alternating sum of the sectors is 0 for an even degree, Demaine and O'Rourke §12.2.1), so a pattern that fails Kawasaki is refused as *does not fold flat*, naming the crease and the gap. *The layers.* A flat-folded state is physical only with a superposition order: for every pair of facets that overlap, which is above (Justin 1997; Demaine and O'Rourke §12.3). It is computed as the literature on flat-folded states does (Bern and Hayes 1996; Akitaya et al. 2016; the method described for Ku's *Flat-Folder*), written from those descriptions. The folded edges of all facets (a crease once, a boundary or cut edge once per facet beside it), collinear pieces that overlap or touch merged into their union, are divided by `arrange` inside a box 10 % larger than the folded sheet: its faces are the *cells*, each covered by the facets that contain a point inside it (the middle of the widest stretch of a scan between corner heights, even-odd), its edges the *segments*, with the cells on their two sides; a fold lies along a segment when the segment's middle is inside the crease's folded image. The variables are one bit per pair of facets that share a cell (true: the lower-numbered above). The rules: *adjacency*, the two facets of a crease (seen from the front a valley brings their fronts together: the right facet is above the left one exactly when the crease is a valley and the left facet faces up, or a mountain and it faces down); *taco-tortilla*, at a segment with a fold of facets a and b on one side, every facet through the segment (covering both its sides) is not between a and b, `(x>a) ⊕ (x>b) = 0`; *taco-taco*, two folds at one segment on the same side do not interleave, `(c>a) ⊕ (c>b) ⊕ (d>a) ⊕ (d>b) = 0` (exactly one of c, d between a and b is the interleaving); *transitivity*, no cycle of three among the facets that cover one cell; facets that merely end at a fold's line, on either side (the boundary, a cut, another fold), are not held by it: in the zero-thickness model of the literature a raw edge or another fold may touch a fold's bend (a square twist's pleats do). A first version also held facets on the far side of a fold and three facets meeting at a segment to one order; the review's square twist showed that this refuses flat folds the published model accepts (its pleats overlap in a pinwheel), so both were taken out (2026-10-28). Tortilla-tortilla constrains two unfolded joins at one segment, and the flat state has none (a line left flat is no crease of the fold). The rules are parity conditions and three-literal clauses; they are solved by unit propagation (a parity with one open bit sets it; two equal literals of a clause set the third to differ), first what the rules force before any choice (a conflict there is reported with the facets of that rule), then per connected component of the rules a depth-first search over the open bits in index order, false first, chronological backtracking: deterministic. A component with no assignment is *cannot fold flat with this mountain-valley assignment*, naming its facets. Whether a pattern folds flat is NP-hard (Bern and Hayes 1996), so the work is capped: 2·10⁸ steps (propagations and constraint visits, the overlay's pair tests and point tests among them), 4·10⁶ rules or bits, 32768 folded edge pieces; past a cap the fold fails as *not decided within the work allowed* (`LayersUndecided`), never a guess. The layers: per facet the longest chain of overlapping facets below it (Kahn's order, lowest index first); pairs that overlap in different places may be cyclic, which is physical (no three share a cell) and then the chain is broken at the lowest facet and `cyclic` is said. *The body* is `folded_sheet` of the flat state: every face in the sketch's plane, coincident faces by design, which `topo::check` does not mind (it is about how entities hang together and lie on their geometry); the area is the sheet's. The mesh-level deep check of `document.check` would see the coincident faces as overlaps, so a fold built flat is not searched for self-intersections there (its layer order is that check, exact); the rest of the deep check (shells that touch, loose meshes) runs as for any body (`commands.rs`, `Developed::FlatFold`). No feature is built from such a body: `derive::source_body`, through which every operand body goes, refuses it by name ([`regeneration.md`](regeneration.md) › *Never through itself*, the one exception). *The caps* are checked before what they bound is made: a stack of k facets in one cell adds k(k-1)/2 pairs and k(k-1)(k-2)/6 triples, a segment with k facets and t folds up to t k + t² rules, and the rules held plus what is about to be added must stay within 10⁶ (some 100 MB at the most), every step counted as work (the collinear sweep, the cover of the cells, the rule loops, the search's scan for the next open bit, kept as a cursor), so a pleat of a thousand layers is refused as undecided at once, in little memory (`a_stack_too_high_is_undecided_at_once_without_the_memory`). *The picture.* The feature's result keeps the layers and per face an offset along the sketch's normal, its layer less the fixed facet's times a thousandth of the body's diagonal (less with many layers, all within a fiftieth); the scene moves each face's triangles by it (a position shared by faces with different offsets split) and each edge by the mean of its faces' (`fold_cmds::layered_mesh`), CPU and GPU alike since both draw the scene's mesh. The body, the measures and every export stay exact. *Not reachable is not refused:* a pattern that folds flat need not fold rigidly to it, so a fold slid towards 180 degrees may stop short (not followed, a touch) while the flat state exists; the guide says so.

**Creases from a sketch pattern** (2026-10-27). A crease names a line of the sketch (`e7`), one copy a sketch pattern made of it (`e7[2]`, `e7[1,3]` in a grid: the doc's `CurveIndex` on the crease), or the line and every copy (`e7[*]`, nested patterns included), each copy its own crease with its own pieces; `alternate` with `[*]` flips the kind on every copy whose indices add up to an odd number (the line itself keeps the kind given), so a pleat or a grid whose count is a variable is one entry. A crease that names one copy wins over `[*]` for it. Every copy of a crease's line must be a crease: one left out is refused, naming it (the copies of a construction line were dropped without a word before, so a patterned strip folded as two facets). The kernel's crease ids are the copies' curve numbers; messages and `fold.inspect` name them as the sketch does (`e7[2]`). In the file a crease writes `instance` (`{"at": 2}`, `{"grid": [1, 3]}` or `"all"`) and `alternate` only when they are set, so files without them are byte for byte as before; the format version was raised to 45 all the same (`migrate::v44_to_v45`, a step that changes nothing), as for every field before it, so that an older build says a newer 3DCraft is needed instead of refusing an unknown field. A fold of version 38 to 44 whose crease line has pattern copies, which were dropped without a word, now fails naming them: the rule changed, not the file.

**Measures** (2026-10-27). `kawasakiFails` and `maekawaFails` of a fold count the interior vertices `fold.check` finds failing (counts, `eq 0`), with the honest note; `flatFoldable` (a flag) reads the layers the fold was built with when it is folded flat, and otherwise folds the pattern flat with its kinds as above, whatever the fold's angles are now: true with an order, false for *does not fold flat* or *cannot fold flat* with the reason in the note, an error (never a guess) when the search ran out of work.

**Kawasaki and Maekawa** (`checks.rs`, `fold.check`). At every interior vertex (every edge there a fold crease; vertices on the boundary have no condition, vertices on a cut are listed apart and not checked), with its creases counter-clockwise: Kawasaki (also Justin), the sectors taken alternately add up to π each; it holds when the first sum is within the angular tolerance plus the angle the linear tolerance makes at the shortest crease there (a point may lie anywhere within the linear tolerance), and an odd number of creases never holds. Maekawa: |M − V| = 2. Each vertex reports its sums, how far off, M, V and the difference. **Both are necessary for a vertex to fold flat and neither is sufficient**: a vertex also needs a mountain–valley assignment that meets the big-little-big condition, and a pattern whose every vertex passes both may still not fold flat, because whether a crease pattern folds flat is NP-hard (Bern and Hayes 1996). The command's summary, its result (`note`, `message`) and the guide say so; nothing in 3DCraft claims a pattern folds flat.

**The crease pattern as a drawing.** `file.export` with `fold` writes the flat pattern in the sketch's axes: the outline on the layer `boundary` (black, continuous), mountains on `mountain` (red, dash-dot), valleys on `valley` (blue, dashed), cuts on `cut` (black), each crease line whole, dashes a sixtieth of the outline's diagonal. The drawing crate gained the dash-dot line (`Layer::dot`: DXF line type `DASHDOT`, dash, a quarter gap, a dot, a quarter gap; SVG `stroke-dasharray` of four, the dot a tenth of the dash); a drawing without one is written byte for byte as before.

## The rules this adds, and why each is general

- *Split where a segment crosses or ends on another* (crossing parameters further than the tolerance from the ends; ends within the tolerance of a segment): the definition of the arrangement, for any straight segments.
- *Points within the tolerance are one vertex; a chained cluster wider than four tolerances is refused*: the document's tolerance decides what coincides; a cluster that wide means the pattern is finer than the tolerance can tell, which no rule could resolve.
- *Two touch-points more than a tolerance apart means two segments lie along each other*: true for straight segments of any direction.
- *A cycle of area at most the tolerance times its perimeter* is a floating crease (no area) or a sliver thinner than the tolerance: the tolerance as a width.
- *Hole of another piece only*: a property of planar graphs (a connected piece's own cycles share its vertices).
- *A first step of 1 degree, then up to 10; the first step's guesses at 1, 3, 10, 30, 100 times the step and its half-step test (within a quarter), also asked at the flat sheet when every given angle is 0; later steps accepted when the corrector lands within a quarter of the tangent's prediction; at most 24 halvings; the kinds' penalty weighted by the sheet's size; 60 iterations per step; a work budget of 2·10¹⁰*: these decide what is accepted: the fold is the continuation from the flat sheet along a branch, scale-free (fractions of the step and of the predicted motion, never a speed); a fold that cannot be followed, reaches a bifurcation or needs more work than the budget is refused with the reason, not guessed.
- *Two solutions of the first step are one solution found twice where their difference is within the tolerances' angle, or within the sum of their error bounds where that is proven one solution (below); past it, two branches in each solved angle whose difference shrinks with the step (at half or at a quarter less than three quarters as large: linear, quadratic for branches tangent at the flat sheet, the square root for a pitchfork); a quarter step that cannot be solved counts as two (refused)* (2026-10-28; after review, the first version asked for linear parting and merged the others, the second solved on to 10⁻¹⁵ of the size, below what rounding allows on long loops, the third merged on existence alone): the first step's solves are polished past the stopping rule while the gap at least halves an iteration, so each stops where rounding of the loops' transforms sets its gap, whatever the size and the loops' lengths. *Existence:* the loops' rows are redundant but consistent (every loop closes at a solution), so the zero-residual Gauss–Newton form of Kantorovich's theorem applies: with `σ` the derivative's smallest singular value from below (`σ² = λ_min / 1.01 · min ‖J eᵢ‖²`, `λ_min` of the unit-scaled normal equations by inverse iteration, which estimates it from above, hence the 1.01) and `L` its Lipschitz constant from above, where `h = L ‖r‖ / σ² ≤ ½` a solution lies within `r = 2 ‖r‖ / σ` of the solve (its error bound), whatever way the solve stopped (a linear rate above a half near a singular derivative also stops the polish). `L = 3 (size + R) √Σ_r k_r² (k_r + 1)²`, `k_r` a row's unknowns and `R` the largest bend radius: an entry `D × (X − C)`, and for a bend `−sign(ρ) R rot(û)`, moves by at most the distance along its loop (`k + 1` bodies of at most the size) plus the radius, per radian of each other joint of the loop (one-sided at a bend's ρ = 0, where the velocity is continuous). *Uniqueness:* existence alone merges nothing, since two solutions may lie as little as `2σ/L` apart and two radii at `h = ½` add up to just that. The solution near a solve is the only one within `σ / L` of it (Kantorovich's ball of uniqueness, at least `1/(βL)` with `β = 1/σ`); so with `a` the solve of the larger `σ` and `d ≤ r_a + r_b` their difference, the solution near `b` lies within `d + r_b ≤ r_a + 2 r_b` of `a`, and the two are one solution where `r_a + 2 r_b < σ_a / L` (`merge_radius`; a case at `h = 0.4` is not merged, unit-tested). Nothing is proven (the bound explains no difference) for a solve that ran out of iterations or stopped above its goal, where `λ_min` is at most the rank pivot or inverse iteration does not settle, where `h > ½`, and for anything not finite: every doubt is two branches, refused, never merged. The shrinking test then only names which angles part (`error_bound`, `merge_radius`, `parting_angles`, unit-tested). The half and quarter solves of a candidate start from half and a quarter of its own solution. The Miura sheet of 3 × 12 cells, whose two solves of one branch stopped at the goal lay 4.5·10⁻⁸ rad apart, folds (`fold_miura`): polished, its duplicates are merged by the tolerances' angle or by a proven radius.
- *Rank pivot 1e-10 on unit-scaled columns*: numerical rank; a crease left free at the solution is refused, never guessed.
- *At the flat sheet (every given angle 0) a rank drop is freedom only when a branch leaves it with the given angles kept at 0*: the first step's own test (the kinds' guesses at 1 to 100 times the first step, a solution at least a quarter of that step from the flat sheet that, solved again from its half, stays at half within a quarter). At the flat sheet every crease through a vertex turns about an axis in the sheet's plane, so the loops' derivative loses rank at every vertex whether or not the vertex can move; the rank cannot tell, the branch test can, for any pattern (2026-10-27, below).
- *Dense up to 400 unknowns, sparse past it and from 32 unknowns wherever the sparse factor costs under a quarter of the dense one; at most 16384 unknowns, 2·10⁷ skyline entries, 4096 creases, 4096 boundary pieces*: the first is a choice of method, both general: the dense solve pivots, the skyline eliminates in a fixed order, so the quarter is a margin in favour of the pivoted dense solve, which gives way only where the sparse one is clearly cheaper (below 32 unknowns the dense factor costs some 11 000 multiplications, no concern to the budget), not a number fitted to a pattern (the first step's worst case, 5 × 2⁶ guesses on a Miura sheet of 311 angles, exceeded the budget dense and takes a small part of it sparse); the others are caps on the work and memory, refused with the reason beyond.
- *The sparse loops: the shortest cycle through each joint, shortest first, kept when independent over GF(2), completed by the fundamental cycles*: a basis of the cycle space of any graph, by construction (the fundamental cycles alone are one).
- *A sparse rank pivot of 1e-10 on unit-scaled columns in the fixed order, and in both tests, when a pivot was dropped or the smallest kept is below 1e-4, the free unknowns named by the near-null directions (inverse iteration on the factor shifted by 1e-10, each direction orthogonal to those found, kept while its eigenvalue is at most 1e-10, up to one more than the elimination found and at most 16; the unknowns carrying a tenth of a direction's largest component)*: for a semi-definite matrix a zero pivot in any symmetric order is a dependent column, but an elimination, unpivoted or pivoted, can miss a near dependence (Kahan's matrix: smallest unpivoted pivot 2e-6, smallest eigenvalue 4e-15); the eigenvalue cannot, and the directions do not depend on an elimination's order: dense and sparse ask them of the same matrix and name the same creases (the names of an undetermined fold changed with it: the creases the free motion moves, not the last ones an elimination left) (`a_near_dependence_no_pivot_shows_is_undetermined_dense_and_sparse_alike`).
- *A cut with one facet on both sides is a slit or a slot; a cycle of no area made of cuts alone is a hole of the facet round it*: what a cut that divides nothing is, for any pattern; a fold crease so is still dangling.
- *Thick panels: set back by the clearance from every crease and cut, mitred; refused where an edge turns or vanishes, the loops cross, a hole falls out, collinear edges differ or turn back; a clearance above the tolerance*: the offset of any polygon, valid exactly when those hold; no fixed angle or ratio anywhere (the limit 2 atan(c / T) is a consequence, tested, not a rule).
- *Thick panels meet when a face of one meets a face of the other (the facet test) or a corner of one lies inside the other*: two solids meet exactly so.
- *A stack: the 180-degree creases (a crease line in or out as a whole) folded flat on their own; a stack folded on when a given angle is neither 0 nor 180 beside a 180; facets of one stack not compared; different stacks may touch along a stretch both have an edge of a stack-folding crease on*: the rigid motion of a 180-degree crease is a fixed half-turn, so the rest is a linkage of rigid stacks; the touch is the zero-thickness model's bend of layers round one line.
- *Coplanar when the sine of the angle between the normals is within the angular tolerance and the corners within the linear one*: the document's tolerances.
- *The solve about the middle of the sheet's box, its stopping rule (10⁻¹³ of the box's diagonal, or a thousandth of the tolerance) and penalty by the box's diagonal*: scale and place do not change the answer.
- *Kawasaki's slack: the angular tolerance plus the linear tolerance over the shortest crease*: what the two tolerances allow at that vertex.
- *Folded flat when every given angle is within the angular tolerance of 180 degrees; a given 180 beside another angle refused*: the flat state is where layers are defined; anything between would be layers of a stack folded on, which is owed, not guessed.
- *Loops of the flat state closed within the linear tolerance at the crease's ends and a point its length beside it*: the rigid solve's own closure test.
- *Collinear folded pieces merged when every end lies within the tolerance of the other's line and they overlap or touch within it; the overlay box 10 % larger*: what coincides is the document's tolerance; the box only has to hold everything.
- *The layer search capped at 2·10⁸ steps and 4·10⁶ rules or bits*: the general problem is NP-hard; past the cap the answer is undecided.
- *The display offset a thousandth of the diagonal per layer, all within a fiftieth*: a picture choice, never in the body.

## Limits, refusals and what is owed

- **Straight creases only.** A curved crease bends the facets beside it, which rigid folding does not do (refused, saying so). The general method, owed: curved-crease origami (Huffman 1976; Fuchs and Tabachnikov 1999; Demaine et al., "Curved crease folding: a review on art, design and mathematics", 2015): the sheet beside a curved crease is a developable surface, ruled, whose rulings meet the crease from both sides; the crease is a space curve whose curvature κ, torsion and the fold angle relate by `κ_g = κ cos(α)` (κ_g the crease's curvature in the flat sheet, α half the dihedral angle between the two sides' tangent planes), so the folded crease and the two rulings fields are solved together (a boundary-value problem along the crease, with the rulings ending on the boundary or other creases without crossing). That needs the developable surfaces of `developable.rs` driven by a crease curve rather than two rails, and a facet that is no longer planar: a new body builder (ruled faces), a new contact test (curved faces), and a solve whose unknowns are functions along the crease. Out of reach for R1; refused by name.
- **A sheet bounded by straight lines.** An outline with arcs or splines is refused, naming the curve. The general method, owed: the arrangement of straight creases with a curved boundary (each boundary curve split where creases end on it, by line–curve intersection; facets whose loops hold curved pieces; their area by Green's theorem over the pieces), and everything that takes a facet as a polygon taking it as a loop of curves: the contact test (a facet's part of a line through it by line–curve intersections), the layers' overlay (curved folded edges), the sheet builder (arc and spline edges on planar faces, as `extrude::fill` already makes) and the thick panels' set-back (the offset of a curve, `offset_profile.rs`). Rigid folding itself does not change (the hinges are straight).
- **Thickness: the membrane model only.** Thick panels with the hinges on the sheet (above), so they fold as the sheet does and meet past `2 atan(c / T)` towards their side; panels folded flat pass through each other and are refused. The offset-panel and tapered-panel methods (hinges moved off the sheet so thick panels fold flat) are owed; a slit or a slot in a thick panel, and a crease running straight into the boundary, are refused (no width, a step).
- **Layers of a stack folded on** are built (above) when the 180-degree creases fold flat on their own; a stack whose 180-degree creases need solved creases at 180 too to fold flat is refused as not flat (give those creases 180), a stack-folding crease at exactly 0 beside another may meet as coplanar stacks, and the order of the layers where a stack bends round a line is not checked beyond the zero-thickness touch (any order is geometrically possible at no thickness).
- **Slits and slots** are built (above); in a flat fold a facet's slit does not let another layer through it (one bit per pair of facets).
- **The sparse solve**'s skyline grows with the bandwidth reverse Cuthill–McKee finds (some √n per row for a grid), so its work grows about as n² for an n-angle grid (0.9 s at 3119 angles); a nested-dissection order would do better on very large patterns; past the work budget or 2·10⁷ entries a fold is refused with the reason.
- **Pattern instances.** Creases are lines drawn in the sketch, not instances of a sketch pattern (their curves have no entity of their own); a Miura sheet is drawn line by line (or by a script).
- **The branch.** The solve follows the branch the mountain–valley assignment picks from the flat sheet; a configuration reached only through a flat-folded state (past a bifurcation) is not looked for; a bifurcation partway is refused as undetermined, by name.
- **Zero angles with solved creases** fold to the flat sheet when the given angles hold it there (a degree-4 vertex or a Miura sheet driven at 0), and are undetermined when a fold can leave it with them kept at 0 (the waterbomb with only its midline at 0: a diagonal folds alone) or when no angle is given at all.
- **Zero thickness.** The rules are those of the zero-thickness model (bends may touch raw edges and other bends from outside); paper of real thickness may need more room than an accepted order gives.
- **One bit per pair.** A pair's order is one bit for the whole pair, as in the literature; two non-convex facets (an L-shaped outline, a facet with a hole) that overlap in two places apart could in principle lie the other way round in each, which one bit cannot say, so such a pattern may be refused although it folds.
- **Nothing is built from a flat fold** yet (a transform, a pattern, a plate of it): refused by name, owed.
- **Flat-foldability** is decided for a given mountain-valley assignment (the layer search; big-little-big and Maekawa fall out of it), within a work cap; past it the answer is *undecided*. Finding an assignment that folds flat (the kinds left open) is not looked for.
